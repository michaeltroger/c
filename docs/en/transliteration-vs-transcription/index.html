Transliteration and romanization

Transliteration maps letters from one script to another according to a defined system. Romanization means writing a language that normally uses another script with Latin letters; some standards are designed for passports, place names or bibliographies and may not be reversible.

Transcription and translation

Transcription represents pronunciation, often using spelling conventions from a particular language: Russian Чехов is commonly written Tschechow in German and Chekhov in English. Translation conveys meaning rather than letters or sounds, so Привет becomes Hello. This converter does neither.

Frequently asked questions

Does the converter translate text or transcribe pronunciation?

No. It converts between scripts according to a selected standard. For example, ISO 9 gives Čehov, while a German pronunciation-based transcription such as Tschechow is not available.

Why do standards spell the same name differently?

Each standard follows its own rules and purpose. Чехов becomes Čehov in ISO 9, Chexov in GOST R 7.0.34, and Chekhov in BGN/PCGN 1947.

Can I convert Latin text back to Cyrillic?

Yes, with reversible options such as ISO 9 and GOST 7.79 System B for correctly spelled words. Passport and other one-way standards only convert from Cyrillic to Latin.
